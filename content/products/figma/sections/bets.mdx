This section has one strategic bet in it, not the four a Figma retrospective usually lists. The reason is a rule I hold myself to: a bet needs evidence for the whole chain. The choice, what it was expected to win, what it cost, what happened, and the tension it leaves behind. For most of Figma's history making decisions, I can show the decision but not the intent. And a timeline isn't proof of a strategy.

## The bet that survives: fund AI hard, meter it with credits

**The bet.** Figma put serious money into AI across the platform, bought Weavy, introduced AI credits on every seat in 2025, and started enforcing limits with paid top ups in March 2026.

**What Figma expected, in its own words.** AI is "already a tailwind" and "TAM-expansive," and being able to swap models is a deliberate design goal. That's the company's stated hope, on the record. Not an outcome.

**The cost, disclosed rather than discovered.** This is what makes the chain writable, and it's rare. Figma priced its own bet in public. The 10-K says inference and model costs will hit cost of revenue, R&D, and sales and marketing, and that the company **expects this to push gross and operating margins down** <Evidence id="ai-margin-warning.fy2025" />.

**What's happened so far.** Cost of revenue rose 112% against 41% revenue growth in fiscal 2025, with $49.1 million of that going to AI infrastructure <Evidence id="cost-of-revenue.fy2025" />. Gross margin went from 88.3% <Evidence id="gross-margin.fy2024" /> to 82.4% <Evidence id="gross-margin.fy2025" />. I can't pin the whole margin move on this one bet. But the direction matches what Figma said would happen.

**The tension now.** The same filing admits pricing power "may be constrained by competitive dynamics," and names AI driven design to code tools and customers' own internal builds as competitors. The technology Figma is funding is also the technology most likely to squeeze it. Status: **unresolved**, which is honest, because the bet is still being placed.

## The candidates that didn't make it

**Browser native architecture (2015)** and **multiplayer collaboration (announced for 2016)** are evidenced as decisions. They're also evidenced as *separate, sequential* decisions <Evidence id="multiplayer.announced.2016" />, which is a finding in itself. But no source from the time tells you what advantage was expected, what trade-off was weighed, or what result management credited to either. Writing those chains would mean inventing reasons for choices made a decade ago by people who never explained them.

**The dual class structure with concentrated founder control.** 72.3% of voting power at the end of 2025 <Evidence id="voting-power.field" />. A documented arrangement, and Figma itself lists the downside as a risk factor. But why it was chosen, and what it was traded against, isn't established.

<Judgment>
There's an asymmetry here worth saying out loud. The only bet with a complete evidence chain is the one Figma made while it had to disclose things publicly. The historically interesting bets are the least evidenced. Not because they were less deliberate, but because private companies never have to show their reasoning. Any teardown that narrates the 2012 to 2015 decisions as confident strategy is writing fiction with good production values.
</Judgment>
