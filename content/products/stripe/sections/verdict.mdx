## What the evidence establishes

A price that hasn't moved in fifteen years: 2.9% plus 30 cents at launch <Evidence id="pricing.launch.2011" /> and today <Evidence id="pricing.us.2026" />, with the surcharges around it raised in 2023 <Evidence id="pricing.change.june-2023" />. A product stack in which every addition is priced as a further slice of the same payment flow <Evidence id="pricing.products.2026" />. A consumer wallet that enrols people with Stripe rather than with any merchant <Evidence id="docs.link.enrolment" />, and an export policy that will hand back every saved card except the ones saved through that wallet <Evidence id="docs.pan-export.link-exclusion" />. One audited window, a UK subsidiary with £896.5 million of turnover <Evidence id="spuk.turnover.fy2024" />, which says nothing about the group's margin. And a $53 billion bid for PayPal, made and withdrawn inside six weeks <Evidence id="paypal.bid.timeline.2026" />.

## What the evidence suggests

<Inference>
Reported revenue of $5.1 billion on $1.4 trillion of volume <Evidence id="revenue.2024.reported" /> <Evidence id="tpv.2024" /> is a yield near 0.4%, which says the headline rate is what small businesses pay and not what Stripe earns. The growth that Stripe's letters describe, a Revenue suite going from $500 million toward $1 billion <Evidence id="revenue-suite.run-rate.2026" />, comes from the stack, not the rail. And free cash flow of about $3.2 billion <Evidence id="fcf.2025.reported" /> is what lets Stripe buy its way into stablecoins, metering and model routing without asking anyone. Each of those is a reported figure from a second-hand source, consistent with the others, and not audited by anyone.
</Inference>

## What stays open

Group margin. Group headcount since 2022. Any market share. What Stripe paid for Bridge, Privy, Metronome or OpenRouter. What happens to a business between its first charge and meaningful volume, which is the one stage where the only independent evidence is people describing holds. Whether Link's non-portability has ever actually cost a departing merchant. And what enterprises hire Stripe for, in their own words.

## My judgment

<Judgment>
The central call in this teardown, mine and not a fact: **Stripe still sells the 2.9% + 30¢ it launched with in 2011. What changed is everything sold on top of it, and a consumer wallet that quietly makes leaving harder than arriving.**

Three sentences that need to stay apart. "Stripe's headline price is unchanged" is verified, from two sources fifteen years apart. "Stripe's revenue growth comes from products attached to payments" is an inference from public pricing and Stripe's reported run rates, and I'd defend it. "Link is Stripe's moat" is a judgment built on a mechanism Stripe documents and a consequence nobody outside Stripe has yet reported feeling. The first two are the teardown. The third is the thing I'd most want to be argued with about.

What would change my mind, in order: a merchant's account of leaving Stripe and losing Link-saved customers, which would turn the moat from stated to observed; an audited group figure, from the Irish consolidated accounts or an eventual filing, which would test the 0.4% yield; and any independent measurement of a transition in the loop, which would let one dashed link become solid.

A note on how this verdict was reached. It leans on Stripe's own documents more than I'd like, because for a private company the documentation is the best evidence there is. Stripe's letters and docs are specific and, in places, self-critical, and I've used them as what they are: the company describing itself, well.
</Judgment>

## Open questions

The five that shape this verdict most: whether Link's lock-in bites in practice, what group margin actually is, what happens between first charge and scale, what the acquisitions cost, and whether any agentic or stablecoin surface carries volume yet.
